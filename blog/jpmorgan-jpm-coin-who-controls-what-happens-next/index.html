JPMORGAN IS PUTTING BANK DEPOSITS ON BLOCKCHAIN. WHO CONTROLS WHAT HAPPENS NEXT?

Public blockchains can move bank-issued money. Regulated institutions still need to govern the decisions that put it in motion.
October 20, 20269 min readInstitutional Infrastructure

On October 19, 2026, JPMorgan made a consequential move: it brought JPM Coin, its dollar-denominated deposit token, to Ethereum mainnet. Not another slide about the future of finance. An institutional bank deposit represented on a public blockchain.

JPM Coin (JPMD) was already available to eligible institutional clients on Base, an Ethereum layer-2 network. JPMorgan has also announced a phased expansion to Canton. The distinction matters: Ethereum and Base availability is not the same thing as a completed Canton rollout. But the direction is unmistakable. Bank money is becoming programmable across multiple networks.

JPMorgan has shown where institutional digital money is heading. The harder question for the rest of banking is: who governs it when it gets there?

A BANK DEPOSIT IS NOT JUST ANOTHER STABLECOIN

JPMD represents U.S. dollar deposits at JPMorgan and is restricted to eligible institutional participants. It is not interchangeable, legally or operationally, with a conventional payment stablecoin issued against a separate reserve portfolio. Each instrument brings different issuer exposures, contractual rights, permitted users and oversight considerations.

What they share is a new operating reality: value can move on blockchain infrastructure around the clock. Tokenized deposits can support institutional payments, settlement and collateral workflows; payment stablecoins and tokenized securities can involve other issuers, networks and legal structures.

To an institution, more available rails do not automatically mean more available controls. A network can record the transfer. It cannot, by itself, establish the bank's internal business authority for initiating that transfer.

THREE NETWORKS. ONE INSTITUTIONAL QUESTION.

1
Base — a public layer-2 starting pointJPMD became available on Base in November 2025, demonstrating a bank-issued deposit token in a public-network environment for eligible institutional use.
2
Canton — a privacy-oriented expansion planAnnounced in January 2026, the Canton initiative is structured as a phased effort around issuance, transfer and redemption. An announced expansion is not proof that every phase is complete.
3
Ethereum — institutional money meets the mainnetOn October 19, 2026, JPMorgan announced JPMD's availability on Ethereum mainnet, citing Ethereum's tokenized-asset ecosystem and onchain liquidity.

Networks differ in their execution models, permissioning, privacy and operational constraints. Yet institutional decision-making must endure as infrastructure changes: Who may act? Under whose authority? Which limits apply? What must be reviewed? Which evidence survives?

WHAT A TRANSACTION HASH DOESN'T TELL YOUR EXAMINER

Imagine a community bank using digital-asset rails to settle an approved tokenized-asset transaction. The onchain record may show that a token moved between addresses. It does not necessarily reveal whether the transfer was initiated by an authorized employee, met the institution's counterparty criteria, respected exposure limits, cleared required compliance checks or matched its approved purpose.

That information exists in the institutional decision, not simply in the settlement receipt. And an institution should be able to reconstruct both, together, after the fact.

In our September analysis of stablecoin controls, we put it this way: the rail executes; the institution owns the decision; the audit record connects them. JPMorgan's expanding footprint makes the principle more relevant, not less.

THE JPMORGAN ADVANTAGE ISN'T AVAILABLE OFF THE SHELF

JPMorgan has the institutional scale to build and operate its own digital-asset products, governance and control infrastructure. Smaller institutions don't need to copy every component of that model. They need a disciplined way to decide which digital-asset activities they can support, under what authority, through which providers and with what evidence.

A credit union or community bank evaluating tokenized deposits, stablecoin-based settlement, digital-asset collateral or tokenized debt should be asking practical questions before choosing a chain or issuer:

AUTHORITYWho is eligible to request, approve, revoke or execute each action?
POLICYWhich institution-defined limits, checks and exceptions must be satisfied?
EXECUTIONHow does an authorized decision reach a supported wallet, OMS or settlement provider?
EVIDENCECan request, decision, execution, exception and reconciliation be connected later?

For smaller regulated institutions, the opportunity is to participate in this evolving market without surrendering the authority, governance and customer relationships that define their role.

THE REAL TEST IS AN APPROVED TRANSACTION — AND A DENIED ONE

Picture a proposed tokenized-asset movement at a community bank. A properly authorized institutional user submits it. The control layer verifies the role and intended workflow, evaluates policy, records the approval and permits a supported execution route. The institution then reconciles what actually happened with what was approved.

Now change a critical condition: the amount exceeds a policy limit, the user lacks authority or a required dependency cannot be verified. The execution should not quietly proceed. The system should preserve an understandable denial or exception record.

The distinction is foundational: the value of institutional infrastructure lies not only in enabling transactions, but in governing when—and under what conditions—they occur.

THE BIG BANKS HAVE STARTED MOVING. COMMUNITY FINANCE STILL HAS A CHOICE.

The industry's next contest won't be settled by who says “blockchain” most often. It will be shaped by who can connect new forms of money to the governance standards and customer relationships that regulated finance already depends upon.

For credit unions, community banks and their technology partners, there is a sensible sequence: choose a real institutional workflow; define policy and decision rights; select a lawful, supported execution route; test both authorized and denied paths; retain the evidence; measure whether the result improves the institution's operations or economics.

That is more useful than attempting to recreate JPMorgan. It also leaves room for multiple issuers, networks and service providers rather than locking the institution's rules inside any one of them.

The future of digital money will have more than one rail. Institutional accountability cannot be optional on any of them.
