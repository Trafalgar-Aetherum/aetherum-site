CREDIT UNIONS ARE ENTERING THE DIGITAL-ASSET MARKET STRUCTURE CONVERSATION

The latest CLARITY Act language is bigger than another Washington crypto development. It raises a more consequential question for America's credit unions: who will control the infrastructure connecting trusted financial institutions to the digital-asset economy?
September 13, 20268 min readStrategy & Research

For years, the conversation around credit unions and digital assets has largely been framed as a question of permission.

Can credit unions participate?

Can they offer members access to digital assets?

Can they work with digital-asset providers without creating unacceptable regulatory, operational or reputational risk?

That conversation is beginning to change.

The latest evidence came from Washington. CoinDesk reported that revised Digital Asset Market CLARITY Act language includes changes affecting decentralized finance and, notably, credit unions.

Whether the legislation ultimately advances is important. But for credit-union leaders, it may not be the most important development.

The larger signal is that credit unions are now being explicitly contemplated as participants in America's emerging digital-asset market structure.

That deserves attention.

From “Should We?” to “How Do We?”

The legislative development is not occurring in isolation.

Senate Banking Committee materials around digital-asset market structure increasingly contemplate regulated financial institutions—including credit unions—as participants in permitted blockchain and digital-asset activity.

America's Credit Unions has likewise been engaged in the policy debate around how federal and state-chartered credit unions should participate in digital assets.

Meanwhile, the NCUA has made responsible adoption of financial technology and digital assets part of its strategic agenda and continues to address the regulatory questions created by stablecoins and other emerging infrastructure.

At the state level, the movement is already tangible. Minnesota enacted legislation enabling state-chartered banks and credit unions to provide digital-asset custody services beginning August 1, 2026.

Taken together, these developments suggest that the strategic question is evolving.

It is becoming less: “Will credit unions be allowed to participate in digital assets?”

And increasingly: “How should credit unions participate—and on whose infrastructure?”

Credit Unions Have Something Crypto Platforms Cannot Manufacture

The digital-asset industry has spent enormous amounts of capital attempting to manufacture trust.

Credit unions already have it.

They have regulated institutions, established member relationships, compliance organizations, lending expertise, local-market credibility and, in many cases, relationships extending across generations.

That is a formidable strategic position.

But it can be lost if digital-asset adoption simply means redirecting members into someone else's financial ecosystem.

If a member has to leave the credit-union relationship to access digital assets, borrow against those assets, transact with stablecoins or eventually use tokenized financial products, the credit union risks becoming an observer to an increasingly important part of that member's financial life.

The strategic objective should therefore not be to turn credit unions into crypto companies.

It should be to allow credit unions to extend the institutional advantages they already possess into a new financial architecture.

Infrastructure Is Becoming the Real Question

Digital-asset infrastructure for regulated financial institutions requires much more than a wallet and an exchange connection.

Who controls the member relationship?

Who controls identity?

Where are assets held?

Who possesses the keys?

How is a transaction authorized?

How is collateral valued?

What happens when risk thresholds change?

How are AML and KYC controls enforced?

What can an examiner see?

Who can freeze, approve or reverse an institutional action?

How are decisions reconstructed months or years later during an examination or audit?

And perhaps most importantly: Does the credit union remain in control?

These are not cryptocurrency questions. They are financial-infrastructure and governance questions.

That is precisely why the appearance of credit unions in federal market-structure legislation matters.

Regulatory permission without institutional infrastructure does not solve the problem. It simply exposes the next one.

Stablecoins Make the Stakes Even Clearer

The debate over stablecoin rewards illustrates what is really at stake.

Credit-union and banking organizations have warned that deposit-like rewards offered by stablecoin providers could move funds away from regulated depository institutions.

That concern is legitimate.

But there is another way to look at it.

If tokenized dollars and blockchain settlement become meaningful components of financial infrastructure, community financial institutions should not merely defend themselves against them.

They should have a credible way to participate.

The competitive choice should not become traditional banking or digital finance. Credit unions should be capable of delivering both.

And they should be able to do so while maintaining the governance, compliance, member protections and institutional controls expected of regulated financial institutions.

Custody Is Only One Layer

Current NCUA guidance provides an important reminder of how carefully these distinctions need to be made.

Federally chartered credit unions are not currently authorized to serve directly as custodians for cryptocurrencies and other digital assets, although some state-chartered institutions may possess authority under state law. Digital assets themselves are not protected by NCUA share insurance.

At the same time, federally insured credit unions can establish appropriately governed relationships with third-party providers offering digital-asset services to members, subject to applicable due diligence, risk assessment and vendor oversight.

This is precisely why architecture matters.

A regulated institution does not necessarily need to perform every function itself. But it needs to understand—and govern—the infrastructure connecting those functions.

MODULAR INFRASTRUCTURECustody, liquidity, settlement, identity and risk can be supplied by specialized systems without displacing the credit union.
INSTITUTIONAL CONTROLThe credit union remains the financial institution at the center of the member relationship and governance model.

Custody can be provided by an appropriately qualified partner. Liquidity can come from another. Blockchain networks can provide settlement. Identity and compliance systems can establish authorization. Risk engines can govern collateral. Stablecoin infrastructure can provide movement of value.

The opportunity is to orchestrate those components into an institutional operating model in which the credit union remains the financial institution at the center of the relationship.

The Next Competitive Advantage May Be Institutional Control

This is where we believe the conversation needs to go next.

At Aetherum, we have spent the last 19 months working from a straightforward premise:

Credit unions should not have to become crypto companies to participate in digital finance. They need infrastructure that allows them to remain credit unions.

That means designing digital-asset infrastructure around the requirements of regulated institutions rather than asking regulated institutions to adapt themselves to infrastructure originally designed for crypto-native markets.

We believe identity should be institution-controlled.

Compliance should be auditable.

Digital-asset collateral should operate within defined risk policies.

Custody should be integrated without unnecessarily surrendering the member relationship.

Settlement should be interoperable.

And examiners should be able to understand what happened, who authorized it and which controls were applied.

The technology matters. But the control model matters more.

This Is Bigger Than One Vote

The immediate congressional debate should not be mistaken for a final determination of America's digital-asset policy.

The legislation may advance. It may change again. It may stall.

But credit-union leaders should not confuse uncertainty over one piece of legislation with uncertainty over the direction of financial technology.

Congress is discussing credit unions explicitly in digital-asset market structure.

NCUA is addressing stablecoin implementation and responsible digital-asset adoption.

States are beginning to establish custody frameworks.

Credit-union organizations themselves are actively negotiating how their institutions should participate.

Those are not theoretical signals. They are the beginnings of a market structure.

Credit Unions Should Help Design What Comes Next

There is a window in every major infrastructure transition when the rules, partnerships and technology models are still being formed.

That is the moment when institutions have the greatest ability to shape the outcome.

For America's credit unions, that moment may be arriving now.

The objective should not be to chase cryptocurrency adoption.

It should be to ensure that as money, collateral, identity and settlement increasingly interact with blockchain infrastructure, credit unions have an institutional path into that economy that preserves what makes the credit-union model valuable in the first place.

Trust. Control. Member ownership. Prudent risk management. Regulatory accountability.

And the ability to serve members through the next generation of financial infrastructure rather than sending them somewhere else to find it.

The CLARITY Act may or may not ultimately provide all of the regulatory clarity the industry wants. But its latest language has already made one thing clearer:

Credit unions are no longer outside the digital-asset market-structure conversation.

Now they should make sure they have a meaningful role in designing what comes next.
