SMART ACCOUNTS ARE NOT ENOUGH.

ERC-7579-style modularity expands what an account can do. Regulated finance still has to govern who can do it, under which policy, for how long, and with what evidence.
October 4, 20267 min readInfrastructure

Smart accounts are becoming more capable. For regulated financial institutions, capability is only half the problem.

Modular account architectures can separate validation, execution and other account behaviors into components that can evolve independently. That is powerful. It is also exactly why governance becomes the real product requirement.

A credit union cannot simply ask whether a smart account can perform an action. It has to know whether that action is permitted for this institution, this member relationship, this asset, this product and this moment in time.

Modularity expands capability. Regulated finance has to bound authority.

WHAT ERC-7579 CHANGES

ERC-7579 points toward a more modular smart-account model: account behavior can be extended through discrete components rather than being permanently fused into one monolithic implementation.

That opens the door to accounts that are more adaptable, more composable and easier to evolve as infrastructure changes.

But a regulated financial institution sees another consequence immediately: every new capability becomes a new policy surface.

If an account can use different validation paths, execution functions or modular controls, the institution needs a way to govern which capabilities are active, when they are active and what they are allowed to authorize.

THE REGULATED QUESTION IS DIFFERENT

Consumer crypto products often optimize for convenience: make the account more programmable, reduce friction and let the user do more.

A credit union has an additional obligation. It has to preserve institutional authority around that programmability.

That means asking questions such as:

1
Which institution owns the policy?The same technical capability may be acceptable for one institution and restricted for another.
2
What authority has been granted?Permission should be tied to a defined purpose rather than treated as blanket control.
3
How long does that authority last?Operational access should be bounded, reviewable and revocable.
4
What happens when policy cannot be verified?Critical institutional controls should fail closed rather than convert uncertainty into authorization.
5
Can the decision be reconstructed?The institution needs evidence of the policy context, authorization state and resulting execution.

BOUNDED AUTHORITY IS THE KEY

The idea of a session or delegated capability is attractive because it can reduce repeated user interaction and allow approved workflows to operate efficiently.

In regulated finance, however, delegated authority should not become unlimited authority.

The institution may need to constrain a capability by transaction type, asset class, amount, destination, product, time window or other policy context. More importantly, the authority has to be capable of ending.

PURPOSE-BOUNDAuthority exists for a defined workflow, not for every possible action the account can perform.
TIME-BOUNDPermissions can expire rather than becoming permanent background access.
POLICY-BOUNDAn institution's current rules remain part of the authorization decision.
REVOCABLEThe institution can withdraw authority when risk, eligibility or policy changes.

This is a very different mental model from simply asking whether a signature is cryptographically valid. A valid signature answers who authorized a message. Institutional policy answers whether that message should be allowed to execute.

THE ACCOUNT SHOULD NOT BECOME THE POLICY SYSTEM

There is a temptation in blockchain architecture to push every rule into the smart account itself.

For regulated institutions, that can create a brittle system. Policies change. Products change. Risk tolerances change. Jurisdictions change. Vendors change.

The account should be able to enforce institutional decisions without becoming the only place those decisions can be expressed.

That is why the more durable architecture separates policy context from execution control. The account can enforce a decision while the institution retains ownership of the governance model around that decision.

FAIL-CLOSED MATTERS MORE THAN FEATURE COUNT

In ordinary software, an unavailable dependency is often treated as a reliability problem. In financial authorization, it can become a governance problem.

If a required institutional control cannot be evaluated, the safest outcome is not to assume approval. It is to stop the action until the required decision can be made.

This is the same principle that runs through Aetherum's broader architecture: critical policy checks belong before execution, and uncertainty should not silently turn into permission.

When a required control disappears, authorization should disappear with it.

AUDITABILITY HAS TO SURVIVE MODULARITY

Modularity is useful only if the resulting system remains intelligible to the institution operating it.

An examiner or internal reviewer should not need to reverse-engineer a collection of account modules to understand why value moved.

The institution needs a coherent record connecting the relevant account, the governing policy context, the authority in force, the decision made and the resulting transaction.

That does not require exposing sensitive internal logic publicly. It requires preserving enough institutional evidence to explain the action later.

THE CONTROL STACK

This is where modular smart accounts fit into the larger Aetherum model.

The important point is not any one standard. It is the separation of responsibilities.

Standards will evolve. Wallet providers will change. Settlement networks will change. The institution still needs a durable way to own policy, constrain authority and preserve evidence.

SMART ACCOUNTS BECOME FINANCIAL INFRASTRUCTURE WHEN GOVERNANCE CATCHES UP

ERC-7579 and other modular-account approaches can make blockchain accounts significantly more capable.

For credit unions and community banks, the opportunity is larger than programmability. It is the ability to make that programmability institutionally governable.

That means modularity with limits. Delegation with revocation. Execution with policy. Automation with evidence.

Those are the properties that move smart accounts from interesting blockchain infrastructure toward infrastructure a regulated financial institution can actually operate.

The future of smart accounts is not just more capability.

It is capability that an institution can authorize, constrain, revoke and explain.
